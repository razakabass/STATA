## Overview
This project analyzes survey data on willingness to pay (WTP) for climate change abatement using two elicitation formats:
Dichotomous Choice (DC) respondents accept or reject a proposed cost.
Two Way Payment Ladder (TWPL) respondents indicate a minimum and maximum WTP, averaged for analysis.
The study compares mean and median WTP across formats, evaluates statistical differences, and discusses implications for climate policy.

## Key Findings
# Attitudinal Measures
Independent samples t tests showed no significant differences in climate attitudes, pro government intervention, or pro environmental attitudes between DC and TWPL groups. Climate attitudes were marginally different at the 10% level, but overall attitudinal measures appear robust to elicitation format.

# Distributional Insights
- Median WTP is less sensitive to extreme values, making it a robust measure of typical household willingness.
- Mean WTP is more appropriate for aggregate welfare calculations, but its sensitivity to outliers requires caution.
- Both measures should be reported in policy contexts to balance efficiency with equity.
# Policy Context
Leading think tanks estimate that $20 trillion USD of investment in low carbon energy and efficiency technologies is needed by 2030 to meet Paris Agreement targets. This equates to approximately €3,273 per adult in total, or €298 per adult per year (2020–2030).
# Comparison with WTP
- Median WTP (DC: €192; TWPL: €132) is below the required €298 annual contribution.
- Mean WTP (DC: €348; TWPL: €268) is closer to the target, with DC mean exceeding the benchmark.
- Assuming German attitudes reflect global preferences, a tax equal to the median WTP would not fully finance climate mitigation, but the mean WTP suggests aggregate feasibility if higher income households contribute more.
# Recommendations
# 1.	Use Both Mean and Median WTP
- Mean: for aggregate cost benefit analysis.
- Median: for affordability and equity considerations.
- Reporting both ensures transparency and robustness to survey design.
# 2.	Increase Public Support
- Enhance trust in climate policies through clear communication of benefits.
- Use revenue recycling (e.g., rebates, green investments) to offset household costs.
- Promote citizen involvement in climate initiatives to strengthen legitimacy.
# 3.	Policy Implications
- Governments should not treat DC and TWPL formats as interchangeable.
- Survey design influences WTP estimates, so methodological validation is essential.
- Climate financing strategies should balance economic efficiency (mean WTP) with social acceptability (median WTP).
# Conclusion
This analysis demonstrates that elicitation format significantly affects WTP estimates for climate change abatement. While mean WTP under the DC format suggests feasibility of financing climate goals, median WTP highlights affordability challenges. Policymakers should report both measures and design interventions that enhance public support, ensuring that climate financing is both economically viable and socially equitable.
